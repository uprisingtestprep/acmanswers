Chapter 1: Critical Appraisal of Clinical Advances

Question 1
Correct answer: B. The absolute risk reduction is two percentage points, so about two extra strokes are prevented among every 100 patients treated.
Why the other choices are wrong: The 20 percent relative reduction is true but does not show how many patients benefit. One in eight is an approximation of the number needed to treat, not the absolute risk reduction. A final risk of 2 percent is seven percentage points lower than the original risk and is not what the trial reported.
Question 2
Correct answer: C. A favorable surrogate result is useful only when the marker has been validated as a reliable predictor of patient-important outcomes in that setting.
Why the other choices are wrong: Statistical significance in a marker does not prove the drug helps patients. Easier measurement does not make an unvalidated marker a better endpoint. A marker falling is not automatically harmful, so treating every marker decrease as a safety signal overstates what the data show.
Question 3
Correct answer: D. A weak or noncontemporaneous comparator can make the new approach look better for reasons unrelated to the device.
Why the other choices are wrong: Safety is always relevant, but it is not the main problem described here. Historical controls can be useful, yet they do not remove selection bias. A historical comparison does not automatically prove ineffectiveness, so rejection without further analysis is too strong.
Question 4
Correct answer: A. Prediction becomes clinically valuable only when it leads to a timely, effective action and the overall pathway improves patient outcomes.
Why the other choices are wrong: Risk scores can be useful and are not inherently unsuitable for care. Accuracy does not require every possible variable. Anxiety can occur, but it is not the primary issue when no response pathway has been established.
Question 5
Correct answer: B. A convincingly negative trial prevents ineffective adoption and helps patients avoid burden and adverse effects.
Why the other choices are wrong: One negative trial does not end all research. Standard care may still improve with future evidence. Offering an unproven and expensive option on request would not follow from the trial and could cause harm.

Chapter 2: Artificial Intelligence and Clinical Decision Support

Question 1
Correct answer: C. A model must be validated in the setting where it will act, because external performance can change with data and workflow.
Why the other choices are wrong: Original performance does not prove portability. One poor deployment does not invalidate every model. Changing the patient population to fit software would reverse the safe direction of adaptation.
Question 2
Correct answer: D. Calibration compares predicted probabilities with observed frequencies, so a predicted 20 percent that acts like 8 percent is miscalibrated.
Why the other choices are wrong: Large samples generally improve precision rather than invalidate results. A score can create groups that share an average risk without every patient having that exact risk. Ranking can still be useful, even though the displayed probability needs correction.
Question 3
Correct answer: A. A high volume of low-value alerts can train users to dismiss warnings and delay response to genuine deterioration.
Why the other choices are wrong: Attention does not improve when warnings are unreliable. More firing does not improve calibration by itself. Additional alerts can increase automation bias rather than eliminate it.
Question 4
Correct answer: B. Fluent generated text can contain false facts, so an authorized clinician must verify it against primary records.
Why the other choices are wrong: Clear grammar does not establish accuracy. Direct sending removes the review step and can spread the error. Insurance status has no bearing on the safety requirement.
Question 5
Correct answer: C. Average accuracy can conceal groups that are missed or harmed, so subgroup evaluation and monitoring are needed before broad use.
Why the other choices are wrong: Waiting for harm reverses the safety obligation. Removing variables does not guarantee fairness. Patient requests cannot substitute for evidence that the model is safe and effective.

Chapter 3: Precision Medicine and Genomic Testing

Question 1
Correct answer: D. An uncertain variant lacks enough evidence for a definitive diagnosis and should not be treated as a confirmed pathogenic finding.
Why the other choices are wrong: The result does not prove inherited risk or require surgery for relatives. Uncertain findings can later be reclassified, so complete dismissal is unsafe. Treatment still depends on the full clinical context.
Question 2
Correct answer: A. Tumor testing can detect somatic changes that are not present in every cell, so a germline sample is needed to confirm inherited risk.
Why the other choices are wrong: Tumor tissue can contain inherited variants. Germline confirmation does not always alter treatment. Population databases help interpretation rather than make reports unreliable.
Question 3
Correct answer: B. Actionability is only one part of the decision; access, safety, patient goals, and alternatives determine what can be done now.
Why the other choices are wrong: Not every actionable target has an available treatment. An unapproved drug cannot be forced without evidence, safety review, and consent. Deleting a valid result would remove information that may become useful later.
Question 4
Correct answer: C. The right test follows from the clinical question, pretest probability, and the actions that could follow.
Why the other choices are wrong: Broad testing can create uncertainty and burden without a clear plan. Young age does not make uncertain results actionable. Waiting for disease can miss prevention opportunities when a family variant is known.
Question 5
Correct answer: D. Reclassification can change screening, treatment, and family testing, so the record and patient communication must be updated.
Why the other choices are wrong: Old interpretations are not permanently correct when evidence changes. Removing reports destroys the clinical history and undermines trust. Family advice must follow the current evidence and the individual case.

Chapter 1: Critical Appraisal of Clinical Advances

Match Each Appraisal Term to Its Practical Meaning
Absolute risk reduction matches the arithmetic difference in event rates. Relative risk reduction matches the proportional fall in risk. Number needed to treat matches the number treated for about one additional benefit. Confidence interval matches the range of compatible results. Surrogate endpoint matches a marker used in place of a patient-important outcome.
Convert a Trial Result Into Clinical Terms
First result: absolute risk reduction is 5 percentage points, or 0.05, and the number needed to treat is 20. Second result: absolute risk reduction is 1 percentage point, or 0.01, and the number needed to treat is 100. Third result: absolute risk reduction is 5 percentage points, or 0.05, and the number needed to treat is 20. Fourth result: absolute risk reduction is 0.5 percentage points, or 0.005, and the number needed to treat is 200.
True or False: Reading Clinical Evidence
The first statement is false because statistical significance does not measure clinical importance. The second is true because the data remain compatible with both benefit and harm. The third is false because an unvalidated surrogate cannot stand in for a patient-important outcome. The fourth is true because a reliable negative result can prevent unnecessary treatment and harm.
